Volumetric 3D Printer

Mechanical Design and Manufacturing | Additive Manufacturing | Optomechanics
September 2025 - June 2026

This year-long project involved the design, manufacture and testing of a desktop volumetric 3D printer based on Computed Axial Lithography (CAL). Unlike conventional layer-by-layer additive manufacturing, CAL uses a sequence of computed light projections to simultaneously cure a three-dimensional geometry within a rotating volume of photosensitive resin. The project brought together optomechanics, resin formulation, mechanical design, mechatronics and software to develop a complete STL-to-print system.

Background

Computed Axial Lithography (CAL) is a volumetric additive manufacturing technique that differs from conventional 3D printing by forming an object simultaneously rather than layer by layer. A sequence of two-dimensional light patterns is projected into a rotating volume of photosensitive resin, with each projection contributing to the accumulated light dose within the resin. Where this dose exceeds the curing threshold, the resin solidifies to form the desired three-dimensional geometry.

The process applies the principles of computed tomography in reverse. Rather than reconstructing a 3D object from multiple projections, the desired geometry is used to calculate the projections required to create it. This enables rapid, layerless printing and allows parts to be cured around existing components.

My Contribution

My technical contribution focused on the optomechanical system and photosensitive resin, taking both through experimental testing, simulation and design to manufacture, integration and final printing trials. As project manager, I coordinated the concept development, testing and integration of the overall system.

Optomechanics

The optomechanical system was responsible for delivering a focused projection across the resin vat within the dimensional constraints of the printer. Based on the available projector module, the optical configuration was investigated through physical testing and modelling, progressing from preliminary ray-tracing simulations to detailed modelling in Ansys OpticStudio. The resulting optical design was then integrated into the final system through custom-designed and manufactured components.

Optical Simulation

Preliminary ray-tracing simulations were used to investigate potential lens configurations and understand their effect on focal distance and projected image behaviour.

Following the preliminary investigation, a more detailed optical model was developed in Ansys OpticStudio using the projector's optical characteristics. The model was used alongside physical testing to evaluate candidate lenses and refine the optical configuration. Simulation and experimental testing led to the selection of a 250 mm focal length, 50.8 mm diameter plano-convex lens, reducing the required projection distance while maintaining the required projected image size.

Optomechanical Design

Following selection of the final optical configuration, a custom two-part lens mount was designed to position the plano-convex lens 32 mm from the projector lens while maintaining alignment with the optical axis. The mount was manufactured in PETG, selected to accommodate the elevated temperatures around the projector during operation.

A servo-actuated shutter was incorporated into the projection system to prevent unintended resin exposure outside the printing sequence. The shutter physically blocked the optical path before projection began and returned to the closed position immediately after printing.

Optical Testing

Following integration of the optical system, experimental testing was conducted to characterise projection quality across the resin vat and quantify geometric distortion.

Resolution and Focus

A USAF 1951 resolution target was projected at the front, centre and back of the resin vat to assess image quality throughout the printing volume. Resolution was highest at the centre plane, with changes in focus and magnification observed towards the front and back of the vat.

Distortion Characterisation & Correction

Grid projection tests identified barrel distortion within the optical system, with an initial measured TV distortion of −7.94% compared with −2.96% predicted through optical modelling. A Python-based image predistortion algorithm was developed to compensate for the measured deformation prior to projection.

Photosensitive Resin

The resin formulation had to remain sufficiently transparent for light to penetrate the printing volume while providing a controlled curing response at the projector wavelength. Resin development therefore involved formulation, optical characterisation and experimental curing trials to establish a composition suitable for volumetric printing.

Resin Formulation & Characterisation

A photosensitive resin was developed using BPAGDA and PEGDA in a 3:1 ratio, with camphorquinone (CQ) and ethyl 4-dimethylaminobenzoate (E4DB) forming the visible-light photoinitiator system. The formulation was designed around the projector's 455 nm blue-light source.

UV-Vis spectroscopy was used to characterise the optical behaviour of the resin and investigate the effect of photoinitiator concentration on light absorption and penetration through the printing volume.

Curing and Printing Trials

Candidate resin formulations were evaluated through controlled curing tests and iterative printing trials. Early tests were used to investigate curing behaviour and refine the resin composition and exposure conditions, progressing from uncontrolled polymerisation to increasingly well-defined geometries.

Final Results

Integration of the optical, resin, rotation, projection and control systems resulted in a functional desktop volumetric 3D printer capable of completing the full workflow from STL input to physical print. Final testing successfully produced axisymmetric geometries, demonstrating volumetric curing within the rotating resin vat.

Gibbon Crane

Mechanical Design and Manufacturing
September 2024 - June 2025

Background

The Gibbon Crossing Crane was a group engineering project undertaken at Imperial College London, inspired by the New Scientist and LEGO Group's Build the Change competition. The project involved designing, manufacturing and testing a scaled prototype of a crane intended to transport gibbons between forest habitats fragmented by roads.

The proposed solution integrated a manually operated winch, a servo-driven rotating arm and a transport enclosure. The prototype was designed to lift a 3.6 kg load through a minimum vertical distance of 15 cm and transport it horizontally over 25 cm.

Design & Development

The design process involved developing a Product Design Specification (PDS) and conducting morphological analysis to evaluate alternative mechanisms.

The final design incorporated a spring-loaded ratchet winch, a servo-driven rotating arm and an I-beam structure fabricated from EN1A steel. Counterweights were incorporated to maintain balance during operation.

Structural Analysis and FEA

Structural analysis was conducted to evaluate the horizontal arm and upright support under the specified loading conditions. Hand calculations and finite element analysis were used to assess stresses and verify the suitability of the selected structural configurations.

The horizontal arm achieved a maximum von Mises equivalent stress of 2.42 MPa, corresponding to a safety factor of 99. The upright support achieved an equivalent stress of 2.65 MPa and a safety factor of 45.

These results supported the structural design and material selection for the prototype.

The winch incorporated a spring-loaded ratchet mechanism to prevent unintended backward rotation during lifting and transportation.

Finite element analysis was performed on the ratchet gear to investigate local stress concentrations. The simulation results were validated against hand calculations of the expected contact stresses, producing a safety factor of 173 at the identified stress concentration.

The analysis supported the suitability of the ratchet mechanism for the required loading conditions.

Manufacturing and Integration

The prototype was manufactured using conventional machining, CNC milling, sheet-metal bending, spot welding, laser cutting and 3D printing.

An Arduino-controlled servo motor enabled 180° rotation at 5 rpm to minimise load sway. A traffic-light indicator system was integrated to communicate operating status.

The completed prototype successfully integrated the lifting, rotation and transport mechanisms, with individual subassemblies passing testing.

The project demonstrated the integration of mechanical design, structural analysis, FEA, manufacturing and electromechanical control within a functional prototype.

CV Boot Mould

Mechanical Design and Manufacturing
September 2025 - June 2026

Background

Imperial Formula Racing required custom silicone CV boots to accommodate the spacial constraints of the car, where the boot geometry needed to provide sufficient clearance from the surrounding shafts and drivetrain components.

To manufacture the custom geometry, a reusable 3D-printed PLA mould was required. The initial design used a two-piece construction, consisting of an outer mould and an internal core inserted from above. However, after the silicone was cast and cured, the finished CV boot could not be removed without breaking the mould. This made the initial design unsuitable for repeatable manufacture.

Initial Investigation

The initial hypothesis was that the silicone was becoming mechanically trapped by the layer striations on the internal surfaces of the 3D-printed mould. Before redesigning the mould, I investigated methods of improving demoulding, including the use of release agents and surface finishing.

For the second manufacturing attempt, the internal mould surfaces were sanded to reduce surface roughness before a release agent was applied. However, after curing, the silicone boot remained trapped within the mould. This indicated that surface finish alone was not the main limitation and that the mould architecture itself needed to be reconsidered.

Design Iteration

Following the unsuccessful second attempt, I redesigned the mould as a three-piece assembly to allow it to be separated around the cured CV boot rather than requiring the boot to be pulled from a rigid outer mould.

The outer section was divided into two removable halves. Locating features were incorporated into the mating surfaces, with protruding features on one half corresponding to locating holes on the other, allowing the two sections to realign accurately during assembly. he halves could then be clamped together during casting while remaining separable after the silicone had cured.

Final Outcome

The three-piece mould successfully allowed the cured silicone CV boot to be removed without damaging the mould, enabling the mould to be reused for subsequent parts. The resulting custom CV boots were fitted to Imperial Formula Racing's 2026 car.

Reactor Criticality Calculator

Computational Nuclear Engineering
September 2025

Background

This Python programme was developed to calculate the neutron multiplication factor of a reactor mixture and classify the system as subcritical, critical or supercritical. Tkinter was used to develop the graphical interface, JSON to store thermal neutron cross-section data, and Pytest to automate unit and functional testing of the calculation workflow.

Criticality Model

This model assumes one-speed thermal neutrons, zero neutron leakage, homogeneous material composition, constant microscopic cross sections, and no spatial or energy dependence of the neutron flux.

Verification and Testing

Pytest unit tests verify individual calculations, including cross-section conversion, macroscopic cross sections, multiplication factor and criticality classification. JSON-driven functional tests verify the complete calculation workflow using subcritical, critical and supercritical test cases.

Transient Heat Conduction Model

Heat Transfer
March 2025

Background

This Python programme models transient radial heat conduction in a cylindrical resistor with uniform internal heat generation and convective cooling at its outer surface. The heat equation is solved using both Explicit Finite Difference and Crank–Nicolson methods, allowing their numerical behaviour and resulting temperature distributions to be compared. Tkinter was used to develop the graphical interface, while NumPy was used for the numerical calculations and Matplotlib for visualising the transient temperature field.

Numerical Model

The cylindrical heat equation was discretised independently using Explicit Finite Difference Method and Crank-Nicolson schemes, with centreline symmetry and a convective boundary condition applied at the resistor surface.

Results and Visualisation

The independently implemented Explicit and Crank–Nicolson solvers were compared to assess numerical agreement. Results were visualised through radial temperature profiles, two-dimensional temperature fields and an animation of the transient thermal response.

Verification and Testing

Unit and functional tests verify grid generation, Fourier number calculation, both numerical solvers, boundary behaviour, GUI inputs and agreement between the Explicit and Crank–Nicolson solutions. JSON-defined test cases are used to evaluate different physical and numerical configurations.

PWR Thermal-Hydraulics Model

Python Modelling
September 2025

Background

This project involved developing a Python thermal-hydraulics model for the steady-state, single-phase analysis of a simplified Pressurised Water Reactor coolant subchannel. The model calculates coolant flow characteristics, convective heat transfer, channel pressure drop, and axial coolant and clad surface temperature distributions. A Tkinter GUI allows model inputs to be modified and results visualised without changing the source code.

Thermal-Hydraulic Model

The model represents a single coolant subchannel surrounding a fuel rod in a square lattice. Subchannel geometry is used to determine hydraulic diameter and flow area, followed by Reynolds and Prandtl number calculations to characterise the coolant flow.

Convective heat transfer is modelled using the Dittus-Boelter correlation, while a Fanning friction-factor correlation is used to calculate wall-friction pressure drop through the channel.

Axial Temperature and Power Distribution

A cosine axial pin linear power distribution is used to represent heat generation along the fuel rod. A steady-flow energy balance determines the resulting coolant temperature rise, while the clad surface temperature is calculated from the local convective heat-transfer coefficient and heat flux.

Pressure Drop Analysis

Channel pressure loss is calculated from the turbulent Fanning friction factor and the subchannel hydraulic diameter. The model evaluates the cumulative wall-friction pressure drop along the coolant channel.

GUI and Model Outputs

The Tkinter interface allows operating and geometric parameters including fuel-rod dimensions, mass flow rate, coolant properties and inlet temperature to be modified directly. The model then reports key thermal-hydraulic quantities and updates the generated plots.

Outputs include: Reynolds number, Prandtl number, Nusselt number, Heat-transfer coefficient, Pressure drop, Coolant outlet temperature, and Channel temperature rise.

Verification and Testing

Unit and functional tests were implemented to verify individual thermal-hydraulic calculations and the behaviour of the complete analysis pipeline. The current test suite contains 32 tests.

Assumptions and Limitations

The model assumes steady-state, single-phase turbulent flow with constant coolant properties and no boiling, cross-flow, spacer-grid losses or local inlet/outlet losses. It is intended as an engineering modelling tool rather than a detailed reactor-core simulation.

Future development could include temperature-dependent coolant properties, spacer-grid losses and more detailed subchannel modelling.

Guided Wave Defect Detection

Literature Review
October 2025 - December 2025

Abstract

This literature review examined the application of ultrasonic guided waves in characterising structures and detecting defects. The fundamentals of bulk and guided waves were first established, recognising the long-range inspection capability of guided waves due to their confinement by waveguide boundaries. The four main attenuation mechanisms including intrinsic material absorption, scattering, geometrical spreading, and leaky wave losses were then explored. Leaky wave attenuation was identified to dominate in embedded waveguides, with soil porosity, compaction and saturation governing bulk and shear stiffness, thereby influencing impedance mismatch and attenuation magnitude. Attenuation was found to be a function of frequency, while finite element models tended to underestimate field values due to idealised assumptions. Guided wave interactions with defects showed that the refection coefficient increased with defect depth, axial and circumferential extent. Finally, practical considerations including transducer bandwidth, temperature variations and signal processing techniques were discussed.

Milling Machine Automatic Feed

Mechanical Design and Manufacturing
February 2025

Background

This project focused on designing a motorised automatic feed system for the x-axis of a milling machine, aimed at the premium do-it-yourself (DIY) market. The design was built around four main requirements: a high-speed, low-torque motor, power from a rechargeable battery, retention of manual movement, and optimisation for a production run of 10,000 units.

Design Overview

In the final design, the table is supported by a casing housing the complete transmission, including the lead screw, allowing the entire assembly to travel with the table. Rotation of the lead screw against the ball screw, which is fixed to the machine base, drives the table along the x-axis. The feed speed is adjusted using a control knob, while a joystick allows the user to switch between automatic and manual operation.

Transmission Design

Clutch Design

The clutch uses splined shafts and sleeves to enable smooth torque transmission while allowing axial movement.

Material Selection

Lead Screw

Material selection for the lead screw used an Ansys Granta (CES) Ashby map of tensile strength against hardness, reflecting requirements for axial load capacity and wear resistance. High carbon steel was selected for its high tensile strength and hardness, good machinability when annealed, and low cost at the required production volume.

Ergonomics

Ergonomics were considered across the user controls, with the joystick, speed dial and handwheel designed for comfortable and intuitive operation. The joystick controls clutch engagement, the grooved dial adjusts automatic feed speed, and the handwheel enables easy manual positioning of the table.
